\section{Introduction}
This paper presents a mathematical derivation connecting the Space-Time Equivalence (STE), General Relativity\citep{einstein1915}, and Special Relativity\citep{einstein1905}. In doing so, it validates the hypothesis introduced in ``The Universe of Light''\citep{degerlia202501}: that gravitational and velocity-induced time dilation are edge cases of a universal relationship between time and inertia. We build upon DeGerlia's prior work introducing the concept of Inertial Density and predicting time dilation calculations with exact precision\citep{degerlia202505}, which established that the Schwarzschild condition reduces to a constant threshold of mass over mean radius, the DeGerlia Threshold ($D_{crit} = 6.73295 \times 10^{26}$ kg/m), about the selected axis. From these demonstrations, the mathematical equivalence between the STE, General Relativity, and Special Relativity is established, unifying the two theories under a single framework: Inertial Relativity.

The original hypothesis, that time dilation between two systems is determined by the fifth root of their inverse moment-of-inertia ratio, is presented and developed in Sections~\ref{sec:inertial_relativity}--\ref{sec:expressions}. Numerical evaluation across the full parameter space of mass, radius, and density variation (Appendix~\ref{app:pair_table}) shows that this relation is exact under isometric (constant-density) scaling and requires generalization for non-isometric system pairs. The generalized form (Equation~\ref{eq:ste_density}) reduces to the originally hypothesized form in the constant-density limit. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} presents the generalized framework and its consequences.

\section{Inertial Relativity}\label{sec:inertial_relativity}
Inertial Relativity is a principle introduced by the author in a prior paper "The Universe of Light" \citep{degerlia202501}. In that work, we hypothesize that General and Special Relativity emerge from Inertial Relativity. This paper demonstrates that equivalence.

Inertia is relativistic, meaning that without a comparator, any measurement of it would have no meaning. For example, something with a moment of inertia $1 \times 10^{20}$ kg\,m$^2$ might be perceived as difficult to rotate, but not more difficult than something with $1 \times 10^{30}$ kg\,m$^2$. However, the concept of relativity also says that how something is observed is influenced by the frame of observation.
A system, in this context, is any collection of mass and spatial extent one chooses to define. It need not be spherical, uniform, high density, or even massive. A system may be a planet, a uniform sphere, a supermassive black hole, a disc galaxy, a cube, a cubic sample of a cumulus cloud, or a system consisting of a hydrogen proton and a single electron.

\subsection{Rotational Inertia}
Moment of inertia for a system is distinct about every axis in the system. For any given axis, the moment of inertia dictates how an applied torque translates into angular acceleration. Moment of inertia is defined as:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:moi}
I = \sum_i m_i r_i^2
\end{equation}
where $r_i$ is the perpendicular distance from each mass element $m_i$ to the chosen axis. Just as mass governs how a system responds to linear force ($F = ma$), moment of inertia governs how it responds to torque ($\tau = I\alpha$).

\subsection{Linear Inertia}
Linear inertia is mass:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:mass}
M = \sum_i m_i
\end{equation}
where $m_i$ are the constituent mass elements. Linear inertia defines how an applied force translates into translational acceleration. It is quantified entirely by mass ($F = ma$). It is dimensional, operating along a specific spatial vector, but unlike moment of inertia, it is isotropic: mass offers the same resistance to changes in motion regardless of the direction of the vector.

\subsection{Isometric Scaling}
Isometric scaling is the uniform scaling of a system in all spatial dimensions while maintaining constant density. Under isometric scaling, every length in the system scales by the same factor $k$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:length_scaling}
\ell' = k\ell
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ is the characteristic length of the system and $k$ is the linear scale factor. Isometric scaling of a system by a factor of $k$ results in the following changes to mass, radius, and moment of inertia:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:mass_scaling}
M' = k^3 M
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the mass and $k$ is the linear scale factor.

\begin{equation} \label{eq:radius_scaling}
R' = kR
\end{equation}
where $R$ is the mean radius about the selected axis.

\begin{equation} \label{eq:inertia_scaling}
I' = k^5 I
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the moment of inertia about a selected axis; $k$ is the linear scale factor.

\subsection{Inertia Abstracts Mass and Radius}
Because every change in length necessarily changes the system's mass, Equations~\ref{eq:length_scaling}--\ref{eq:radius_scaling} hold only for strict isometric scaling; Equation~\ref{eq:inertia_scaling}, however, abstracts mass and radius into moment of inertia. If two systems share moment of inertia about any axis, then an identical torque applied to each system about that same axis will produce identical changes in motion, regardless of the system geometry; independent of mass and radius individually. The moment of inertia alone dictates how a system interacts with time about a particular axis. From this, we can conclude the following formula is universal and is not bound in any way to isometric scaling. The derivations that follow demonstrate that General and Special Relativity emerge from this principle.

\subsection{Universality}
The extension from isometrically scaled systems to all systems follows from the nature of moment of inertia itself. Because $I$ fully determines rotational response to applied torque, two systems with the same $I$ are dynamically indistinguishable regardless of geometry. A thin shell and a solid sphere with the same $I$ exhibit the same rotational physics.

The fifth root relationship therefore holds universally: for any two systems about any two respective axes, the characteristic length scale factor that determines time dilation is the fifth root of the inverse ratio of their moments of inertia about those same axes. Isometric scaling establishes the exponent (Equation~\ref{eq:inertia_scaling}).

\subsection{The DeGerlia Time Dilation Framework}

Inertial density $P$ is the moment of inertia of a system divided by its volume:
\begin{equation}
P = I/V
\end{equation}
Since linear inertia is mass, this reduces directly to ordinary density $\rho = M/V$ in the linear case; inertial density is the rotational generalization of a quantity already familiar from linear mechanics.

For a uniform sphere, substituting $I = \frac{2}{5}MR^2$ and $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$, this reduces to:
\begin{equation}
P = \frac{M}{R} \times \frac{3}{10\pi}
\end{equation}
Just as ordinary density ($\rho = M/V$) is mass distributed over volume, inertial density is rotational inertia distributed over volume. As stated in the prior work: \textit{mass and density are to linear motion as moment of inertia and inertial density are to rotational motion.} Because $P$ is defined relative to a selected axis, it can be evaluated per axis directly rather than requiring a full tensor treatment when the quantity varies by direction.

\textbf{DeGerlia Compactness} $D = M/R$ is a simplified analog to inertial density, related to $P$ by the geometric factor $3/10\pi$, and represents the mass over mean radius ratio for a system about the selected axis.

Setting $R = r_s$ (the Schwarzschild condition) \citep{schwarzschild1916radius} and solving yields the \textbf{DeGerlia Threshold}: the universal constant of DeGerlia Compactness at which a system undergoes gravitational collapse:
\begin{equation}
D_{crit} = \frac{c^2}{2G} = 6.73295 \times 10^{26} \text{ kg/m}
\end{equation}
The normalized DeGerlia Compactness $D_{norm}$ is the ratio of a system's DeGerlia Compactness to the DeGerlia Threshold:
\begin{equation}
D_{norm} = \frac{D}{D_{crit}}
\end{equation}
$D_{norm}$ is algebraically identical to $r_s/R$ and is therefore a linear scale factor $k$: the value that enters directly into $d\tau/dt = \sqrt{1-k}$. Because $D_{crit}$ is a universal constant, $D_{norm}$ places every system on the same scale, making $D_{norm}$ values directly comparable across systems of any mass. The time dilation follows directly:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\tau}{dt} = \sqrt{1 - D_{norm}}
\end{equation}
Substituting $D = M/R$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\tau}{dt} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{M}{R \cdot D_{crit}}}
\end{equation}

The \textbf{DeGerlia Time Dilation} (DTD) is the square root of the normalized compactness:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dtd}
\text{DTD} = \sqrt{D_{norm}} = \sqrt{\frac{D}{D_{crit}}}
\end{equation}
Since $D_{norm} = k$, the gravitational time dilation is:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:gtd_dtd}
\frac{d\tau}{dt} = \sqrt{1 - \text{DTD}^2}
\end{equation}
and the two quantities satisfy:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:pythagorean}
\text{DTD}^2 + \left(\frac{d\tau}{dt}\right)^2 = 1
\end{equation}
For systems in weak gravitational fields, $d\tau/dt \approx 1 - k/2$; the time dilation signal is confined to digits far from the leading unity, limiting the effective precision of the representation. DTD expresses this signal directly. Because $\sqrt{x}$ is algebraically homogeneous while $\sqrt{1-x}$ is not, DTD ratios decompose into independent mass and mean radius contributions:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dtd_ratio}
\frac{\text{DTD}_1}{\text{DTD}_2} = \sqrt{\frac{D_1}{D_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{M_1}{M_2}} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{R_2}{R_1}}
\end{equation}
The gravitational time dilation ratio $\sqrt{1-k_1}/\sqrt{1-k_2}$ does not factor; the nonlinearity of $f(x) = \sqrt{1-x}$ precludes decomposition into constituent ratios. DTD is defined for all $D > 0$ and diverges as $D \to \infty$, correctly reflecting the unbounded growth of the dilation parameter at and beyond the collapse threshold, where the gravitational time dilation form $\sqrt{1-k}$ is undefined. DTD depends solely on $D/D_{crit}$, requiring no system-specific reference values such as the Schwarzschild radius.

\subsection{$D_{crit}$, $D_{norm}$, and the Role of Ratios}

$D_{crit}$ and $D_{norm}$ play distinct roles in the framework, and the distinction matters for how precision propagates.

\textbf{$D_{crit}$ is a universal constant.} It is the compactness at the collapse threshold, the same value for every system. Its numerical value derives from $c^2/(2G)$ and inherits the experimental precision of $G$ — bounded at approximately six significant figures.

\textbf{$D_{norm}$ is system-specific.} For a given system, $D_{norm} = D/D_{crit}$ is the fraction of the universal threshold that system has reached. Every system has its own $D_{norm}$, determined by its mass and mean radius.

When time dilation is computed for a single system through $d\tau/dt = \sqrt{1 - D_{norm}}$ or $\text{DTD} = \sqrt{D_{norm}}$, the result inherits the precision of $D_{crit}$ — and therefore of $G$. Single-system quantities cannot escape this precision floor.

When time dilation is computed as a comparison between two systems, $D_{crit}$ appears identically in both halves of the ratio and cancels structurally:
\[
\frac{D_{norm,1}}{D_{norm,2}} = \frac{D_1/D_{crit}}{D_2/D_{crit}} = \frac{D_1}{D_2}
\]
The result no longer depends on $D_{crit}$ at all. Comparative predictions are decoupled from the precision of $G$ and bounded only by the precision of the mass and length inputs, which can be measured to many more digits than $G$.

The same property holds for any universal constant. $G$, $c$, and any dimensional combination of universal constants appear identically on both sides of any ratio between two systems, and cancel. The framework's comparative results are therefore robust even to the hypothetical scenario in which $G$ and $c$ are scale-dependent rather than strictly constant: as long as they vary together such that their combinations (and therefore $D_{crit}$) remain identical between the two systems being compared, the cancellation still holds.

This is the structural reason the framework prioritizes ratios. Time dilation is a relational property — the natural object is the comparison between two systems, not the absolute time dilation of one. Single-system quantities require a universal reference to express and inherit that reference's precision; comparative quantities do not. The relational form is both more physically meaningful (no fictional flat-spacetime observer at infinity is needed) and more numerically precise (no inherited precision floor from universal constants).

\section{The Space-Time Equivalence (STE)}
The STE is the core universal equivalence stated by Inertial Relativity. In the original work\citep{degerlia202501}, the author hypothesized:
\begin{quote}
``Ultimately, general relativity, gravitational, and velocity-induced time dilation are phenomena associated with the law of Space-Time Equivalence. They are static-scale frames of reference (from the human perspective), extreme scenarios of velocity or gravity, and the time dilation described by Einstein---these are likely edge cases of Space-Time Equivalence.''
\end{quote}
The universal scale factor between any two systems is the compactness ratio:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:kd}
k_d = \frac{I_1}{I_2} \cdot \left(\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)^3 = \frac{D_1}{D_2} = \frac{M_1 R_2}{M_2 R_1}
\end{equation}
where $I_1, I_2$ are the moments of inertia, $R_1, R_2$ are the mean radii, $M_1, M_2$ are the masses, and $D_i = M_i/R_i$ is the DeGerlia compactness of system $i$. By convention, System 1 denotes the observer and System 2 the observed, consistent with the Schwarzschild convention. The three forms in Equation~\ref{eq:kd} are algebraically identical; $(I_1/I_2)(R_2/R_1)^3$ exposes the inertia-radius structure, $D_1/D_2$ exposes the compactness-ratio structure, and $M_1 R_2/(M_2 R_1)$ is the fully expanded form.

The DTD ratio between two systems follows directly from $k_d$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ste}
\frac{\text{DTD}_1}{\text{DTD}_2} = \sqrt{k_d}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:ste} is the universal STE. The single-system Pythagorean relation $\text{DTD}^2 + (d\tau/dt)^2 = 1$ (Equation~\ref{eq:pythagorean}) provides the complementary GTD form for any individual system; the relationship between DTD and GTD as alternative computational representations is developed in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

Under isometric scaling (constant density), the isometric equivalence holds:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:isometric}
k_i = \left(\frac{I_2}{I_1}\right)^{1/5}
\end{equation}
where $k_i$ is the linear scale factor derived from the inertia ratio. Under isometric conditions the two systems share the same density, and the fifth root of the inertia ratio recovers the linear scale factor exactly. Subsequent references to this specific inertia-derived form use $k_i$.

In the linear context, inertia is $M$ and the linear inertia version of the isometric equivalence applies (see \ref{app:general_ste}). Special Relativity describes linear inertia:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:linear}
k_m = \left(\frac{M_2}{M_1}\right)^{1/3}
\end{equation}
where $M_1$ and $M_2$ are the masses of the two systems and $k_m$ is the linear scale factor derived from the mass ratio. Subsequent references to this specific mass-derived form use $k_m$.

\subsection{Relating Linear Scale Factor $k$ to General Relativity}
Time dilation is always:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:td_k}
\frac{d\tau}{dt} = \sqrt{1 - k}
\end{equation}
where $d\tau/dt$ is the ratio of proper time to coordinate time and $k$ is the linear scale factor derived from inertia. Specific forms include $k_i$ (Equation~\ref{eq:isometric}, isometric scaling) and $k_m$ (Equation~\ref{eq:linear}, static-radius linear case). Every expression of time dilation takes this form; $k$ is always a linear scale factor derived from the relative inertia of two systems about their respective selected axes.

\subsection{Properties of Time}
The STE reveals the following properties of time:
\begin{itemize}
\item Time is relativistic: it has meaning only when compared, and how it appears depends on the frame of observation
\item Time emerges from inertia
\item Time is dimensional in three spatial dimensions
\item Time is multimodal: both linear and rotational
\item Time is dynamic for every system or subsystem
\item Time dilation applies to all systems, not only uniform or spherically symmetric ones
\item Time is universally and precisely determined by the spatial distribution of matter in the system, as captured by inertia, about and along every axis, whether or not in motion or being observed
\item Time dilation is system-relational: no observer experiences their own time as anything but normal; dilation is a structural property of the comparison between two systems
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Physical Interpretation}
The STE states that the relative pace of time for any system about any axis is determined by its moment of inertia about that same axis. The more inertia a system has about that axis, the slower its clock runs relative to a system with less inertia. This is consistent with the notion that time is a measure of change, and inertia resists change. Therefore, the more inertia a system has, the less it changes over a given period of time, and thus the slower its system clock runs relative to a system with less inertia.

At any given time, any system is always undergoing some degree of rotation and some amount of linear motion, however small. This law applies to any system whose mass is distributed over some region of space, requiring, at minimum, both mass and spatial extent. One may define the system as the core of a planet, a disc-shaped galaxy, a cosmic filament, a spherical sample of interstellar space, or even an asymmetric black hole described by the Kerr metric \citep{kerr1963}---a topic for future investigation.


\subsection{STE Example}
Consider a uniform sphere of radius 1\,m and mass 1\,kg scaled isometrically by a factor of two, producing a second sphere of radius 2\,m and mass $2^3 = 8$\,kg. Their moments of inertia are:
\[
I_1 = \tfrac{2}{5}(8\,\text{kg})(2\,\text{m})^2 = 12.8\,\text{kg\,m}^2
\qquad
I_2 = \tfrac{2}{5}(1\,\text{kg})(1\,\text{m})^2 = 0.4\,\text{kg\,m}^2
\]
The ratio $I_1/I_2 = 32 = 2^5$ confirms that moment of inertia scales as $k_i^5$ under isometric scaling. Designating the larger sphere as the observer (System~1) and the smaller as the observed (System~2), the isometric equivalence yields:
\[
k_i = \left(\frac{I_2}{I_1}\right)^{1/5} = \left(\frac{0.4}{12.8}\right)^{1/5} = \left(\frac{1}{32}\right)^{1/5} = 0.5
\]
recovering the linear scale factor between the two systems. The resulting time dilation is:
\[
\frac{d\tau}{dt} = \sqrt{1 - k_i} = \sqrt{1 - 0.5} \approx 0.707
\]

\subsection{Density-Explicit Form}

The STE (Equation~\ref{eq:ste}) can be expressed equivalently in terms of density. For a uniform body $I = \frac{2}{5}MR^2$ with $M = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \rho$, so $I \propto \rho R^5$ and:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ki}
\left(\frac{I_1}{I_2}\right)^{1/5} = \left(\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right)^{1/5} \cdot \frac{R_1}{R_2}
\end{equation}
Substituting into the STE:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ste_density}
\frac{\text{DTD}_1}{\text{DTD}_2} = \left(\frac{I_1}{I_2}\right)^{1/5} \cdot \left(\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right)^{3/10}
\end{equation}
The $3/10$ exponent ($= 1/2 - 1/5$) on the density ratio arises from the difference between the density dependence in the DTD ratio ($1/2$ power) and the inertia ratio ($1/5$ power). Under constant density this factor is unity and $k_i$ (Equation~\ref{eq:isometric}) gives the DTD ratio directly. Equation~\ref{eq:ste_density} is the universal STE; $k_i$ is its constant-density projection. The pair comparison tables (Appendix~\ref{app:pair_table}) demonstrate the universal form's necessity in the non-isometric cases, where the density correction is non-unity.

\subsection{Comparison of Relative Time Paces}
The universal scale factor $k_d$ (Equation~\ref{eq:kd}) is independent of $D_{crit}$: dividing each system's normalized compactness $D/D_{crit}$ cancels the threshold, leaving a direct ratio of compactnesses. This is the \textit{raw} scale factor. When the denominator system is the Schwarzschild condition ($D_2 = D_{crit}$), $k_d$ reduces to the \textit{normalized} linear scale factor:
\[
k = \frac{D}{D_{crit}} = D_{norm}
\]
equivalent to $r_s/R$---the compactness parameter of general relativity. The Schwarzschild case is the specific instance of comparing a system to its own collapse point---same mass, physical mean radius $R$ versus Schwarzschild radius $r_s$.

Applying Equation~\ref{eq:td_k}, $d\tau/dt = \sqrt{1-k}$, converts a normalized linear scale factor into time dilation. The value of $k$ must be normalized ($k = D_{norm}$) for $\sqrt{1-k}$ to yield gravitational time dilation: the pace of time relative to flat spacetime.

\textbf{Property:} The ratio of two normalized values is always raw.
\[
\frac{D_{norm,1}}{D_{norm,2}} = \frac{D_1/D_{crit}}{D_2/D_{crit}} = \frac{D_1}{D_2}
\]
$D_{crit}$ cancels, leaving a direct ratio of compactnesses with no reference to the collapse threshold. A raw linear scale factor cannot be passed into $\sqrt{1-k}$ to obtain a time dilation; it must first be normalized by dividing by $D_{crit}$.

Each system has its own time dilation $d\tau/dt = \sqrt{1 - k}$, where $k$ is that system's normalized linear scale factor. The relative pace of time between two systems is the ratio of their time dilations:
\[
\frac{\sqrt{1 - k_2}}{\sqrt{1 - k_1}}
\]
This is not equal to $\sqrt{1 - k_1/k_2}$, because $k_1/k_2$ is raw---$D_{crit}$ has cancelled---and cannot be substituted into $\sqrt{1-k}$ without renormalization. To recover the linear scale factor from a known time dilation:
\[
k = 1 - \left(\frac{d\tau}{dt}\right)^2
\]
The ratio of the recovered $k$ values yields the raw compactness ratio $D_1/D_2$.

\begin{mdframed}
\textbf{Example (Comparison of Relative Time Paces):} $S_1$: $M_1 = 1 \times 10^{30}$\,kg, $R_1 = 1 \times 10^{7}$\,m. $S_2$: $M_2 = 1 \times 10^{20}$\,kg, $R_2 = 2 \times 10^{-7}$\,m.

\[
k = \frac{M_1 R_2}{M_2 R_1} = \frac{(1 \times 10^{30})(2 \times 10^{-7})}{(1 \times 10^{20})(1 \times 10^{7})} = 2.00000 \times 10^{-4}
\]
Time dilation of $S_2$:
\begin{align}
D_2 &= \frac{M_2}{R_2} = \frac{1 \times 10^{20}}{2 \times 10^{-7}} = 5.00000 \times 10^{26}\;\text{kg/m} \notag \\
\frac{D_2}{D_{crit}} &= \frac{5.00000 \times 10^{26}}{6.73295 \times 10^{26}} = 7.42617 \times 10^{-1} \notag \\
\frac{d\tau}{dt} &= \sqrt{1 - 7.42617 \times 10^{-1}} = 5.07330 \times 10^{-1} \notag
\end{align}
\textbf{Confirmation:} Schwarzschild gravitational time dilation:
\begin{align}
k_2 &= \frac{2GM_2}{R_2 c^2}
= \frac{2(6.67430 \times 10^{-11})(1 \times 10^{20})}{(2 \times 10^{-7})(8.98755 \times 10^{16})}
= 7.42617 \times 10^{-1} \notag \\
\frac{d\tau}{dt} &= \sqrt{1 - k_2} = \sqrt{1 - 7.42617 \times 10^{-1}} = 5.07330 \times 10^{-1} \notag
\end{align}
Relative time dilation between $S_1$ and $S_2$:
\begin{align}
k_1 &= \frac{2GM_1}{R_1 c^2}
= \frac{2(6.67430 \times 10^{-11})(1 \times 10^{30})}{(1 \times 10^{7})(8.98755 \times 10^{16})}
= 1.48523 \times 10^{-4} \notag \\
\frac{\sqrt{1 - k_2}}{\sqrt{1 - k_1}} &= \frac{5.07330 \times 10^{-1}}{9.99926 \times 10^{-1}}
= 5.07368 \times 10^{-1} \notag
\end{align}
\end{mdframed}

\section{General and Special Relativity}
Schwarzschild gravitational time dilation:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:schwarzschild}
\frac{d\tau}{dt} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{r_s}{R}}
\end{equation}
where $r_s = 2GM/c^2$ is the Schwarzschild radius and $R$ is the mean radius about the selected axis.

Lorentz time dilation:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:lorentz}
\frac{d\tau}{dt} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}
\end{equation}
where $v$ is the velocity of the moving frame and $c$ is the speed of light.

These are the same form as Equation~\ref{eq:td_k}, where $k = r_s/R$ in the gravitational case and $k = v^2/c^2$ in the velocity case. The two expressions are equivalent: the escape velocity at mean radius $R$ is $v^2 = 2GM/R$ \citep{goldstein2002}, so $v^2/c^2 = 2GM/(Rc^2) = r_s/R$.

Schwarzschild gravitational time dilation is the STE (Equation~\ref{eq:kd}) evaluated under the constraint $M_1 = M_2$ (same mass, different radii). Lorentz time dilation is the STE evaluated under the constraint $R_1 = R_2$ (same radius, different masses). Both are projections of the same compactness-based relation; the derivations of each projection are developed in Section~\ref{sec:expressions}.


\section{Expressions of Relative Inertial Linear Scale}\label{sec:expressions}

The parameter $k$ is always the linear scale factor between two systems about their respective selected axes. Because $k$ is determined by relative inertia, it is fundamentally an inertial ratio. The various scenarios, such as special relativity, gravitational time dilation, etc., are all directly derived from this inertia ratio. In the case of general relativity, we simply set $M_1$ and $M_2$ equal and set $R_2$ to the Schwarzschild radius (see \ref{app:general_ste}).

Each expression of $k$ below derives from $k_i$ (Equation~\ref{eq:isometric}), where $I_1$ and $I_2$ are the moments of inertia of two systems about their respective selected axes. Substituting the moment of inertia for a uniform sphere, $I = \frac{2}{5}MR^2$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:k_expanded}
k = \left(\frac{\frac{2}{5}M_2 R_2^2}{\frac{2}{5}M_1 R_1^2}\right)^{1/5}
\end{equation}
where $M_1, M_2$ are the masses and $R_1, R_2$ are the mean radii of each system about the selected axis. The geometric constant $\frac{2}{5}$ cancels:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:k_simplified}
k = \left(\frac{M_2 R_2^2}{M_1 R_1^2}\right)^{1/5}
\end{equation}
From here, each derivation applies specific constraints and solves.

\subsection{Static Mass (Gravitational)}
From Equation~\ref{eq:k_simplified}, setting $M_1 = M_2 = M$ and $R_2 = r_s$:
\begin{equation}
k = \left(\frac{M r_s^2}{M \cdot R_1^2}\right)^{1/5} = \left(\frac{r_s^2}{R^2}\right)^{1/5}
\end{equation}
where $r_s$ is the Schwarzschild radius and $R$ is the mean radius of the system about the selected axis. When mass cancels, the degree of homogeneity of the remaining quantity ($R^2$) is $L = 2$, so the exponent reduces from $1/5$ to $1/2$ (see \ref{app:general_ste}):
\begin{equation}
k = \left(\frac{r_s^2}{R^2}\right)^{1/2} = \frac{r_s}{R}
\end{equation}
This is equivalently expressed through inertial density or DeGerlia Compactness, since $P \propto M/R$ and $D = M/R$. When mass is static, the ratio of either quantity reduces to the ratio of mean radii about the selected axis:
\begin{equation}
k = \frac{P_1}{P_2} = \frac{D_1}{D_2} = \frac{R_2}{R_1} = \frac{r_s}{R}
\end{equation}
When compared against the DeGerlia Threshold $D_{crit} = 6.73295 \times 10^{26}$ kg/m:
\begin{equation}
k = \frac{D}{D_{crit}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Static Radius (Linear)}
From Equation~\ref{eq:k_simplified}, when $R_1 = R_2$, $k_i$ reduces to (see \ref{app:general_ste}):
\begin{equation}
k_m = \left(\frac{M_2}{M_1}\right)^{1/3}
\end{equation}
This is Equation~\ref{eq:linear}. The Lorentz factor (Equation~\ref{eq:lorentz}) has the same form as Equation~\ref{eq:td_k}, with $k = v^2/c^2$. Since velocity is distance per unit time, $v^2/c^2$ is the square of a ratio of two lengths, with the shared time unit cancelling. Gravitational time dilation confirms this directly: $v^2/c^2 = r_s/R$ via the escape velocity identity, so the velocity-based $k$ of special relativity and the radius-based $k$ of general relativity are the same length ratio.

\subsection{Constant Density (Isometric)}
When density is held constant, $M \propto R^3$, and the isometric equivalence reduces to a pure linear scale ratio (see \ref{app:general_ste}):
\begin{equation}
k = \left(\frac{R_2^3 R_2^2}{R_1^3 R_1^2}\right)^{1/5} = \left(\frac{R_2^5}{R_1^5}\right)^{1/5} = \frac{R_2}{R_1}
\end{equation}

\subsection{General Case}
When both mass and radius differ between two systems, no component cancels:
\begin{equation}
k_i = \left(\frac{I_2}{I_1}\right)^{1/5} = \left(\frac{M_2 R_2^2}{M_1 R_1^2}\right)^{1/5}
\end{equation}
This is $k_i$ (Equation~\ref{eq:isometric}) without constraint on mass or radius. The static-mass and static-radius cases derived above, producing Schwarzschild and Lorentz time dilation respectively, are edge cases of $k_i$, each arising when one component of inertia is held constant. When densities differ between the two systems, $k_i$ alone is insufficient and the universal STE (Equation~\ref{eq:ste_density}) applies, equivalently expressible as $k_d = D_1/D_2$ (Equation~\ref{eq:kd}).

\subsection{Inertia--Time Dilation Identities}

The preceding cases derive time dilation from inertia. The relationship is invertible: given the DTD ratio and the mean radius ratio, the moment of inertia ratio is fully determined. From Equation~\ref{eq:dtd_ratio}:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:inertia_recovery}
\frac{I_1}{I_2} = \left(\frac{\text{DTD}_1}{\text{DTD}_2}\right)^2 \cdot \left(\frac{R_1}{R_2}\right)^3
\end{equation}
The inertia ratio decomposes into a time dilation factor (the compactness ratio) and a spatial scale factor (the volume ratio). This is the STE (Equation~\ref{eq:ste}) inverted: the $(R_1/R_2)^3$ factor converts the $R^{-1}$ dependence in compactness back to the $R^2$ dependence in inertia.

Each system's DTD can also be computed directly from its moment of inertia. Substituting $M = 5I/(2R^2)$ into $D_{norm} = M/(R \cdot D_{crit})$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dtd_from_I}
\text{DTD} = \sqrt{\frac{5\,I}{2\,D_{crit}\,R^3}}
\end{equation}
This expresses time dilation purely in terms of moment of inertia, geometry, and the DeGerlia Threshold, with no intermediate computation of mass or compactness.

\section{Unification of GR and SR}

From Equation~\ref{eq:td_k}, time dilation is always $d\tau/dt = \sqrt{1-k}$, and $k$ is always the linear scale factor between two systems. In all cases, $k$ reduces to a ratio of characteristic lengths. The Schwarzschild and Lorentz results are not independent discoveries; they are the same expression evaluated under different physical constraints on the same underlying parameter $k$.

As derived in Sections 5.1--5.5, each physical constraint yields a specific expression for $k$: static mass produces $r_s/R$, static radius produces $(M_2/M_1)^{1/3}$, constant density produces $R_2/R_1$, and the unconstrained general case applies the STE (Equation~\ref{eq:ste}). In every case, $k$ reduces to a ratio of characteristic lengths. The velocity-based $k$ of special relativity and the radius-based $k$ of general relativity are connected directly through the escape velocity identity ($v^2/c^2 = r_s/R$). Both classical relativistic results emerge from this single expression without additional parameters, geometric similarity, or domain restrictions.

\section{Analysis and Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The Space-Time Equivalence (Equation~\ref{eq:td_k}) states that time dilation is:
\[
\frac{d\tau}{dt} = \sqrt{1 - k}
\]
where $k$ is the linear scale factor derived from the relative compactness of two systems. Substituting the gravitational case ($k = r_s/R$) directly produces Schwarzschild time dilation (Equation~\ref{eq:schwarzschild}). Substituting the velocity case ($k = v^2/c^2$, related to the gravitational case by the escape-velocity identity $v^2/c^2 = 2GM/(Rc^2) = r_s/R$) directly produces Lorentz time dilation (Equation~\ref{eq:lorentz}). Equivalently, $k = D/D_{crit}$ gives the fraction of the DeGerlia Threshold consumed by the system, and is algebraically identical to $r_s/R$. Both Schwarzschild and Lorentz time dilation are produced exactly by the STE, with no free parameters.

\subsection{Generalization of the Original Hypothesis}

The original hypothesis stated that time dilation is determined by the fifth-root inertia relation $k_i = (I_2/I_1)^{1/5}$. Evaluation against the pair comparison data (Appendix~\ref{app:pair_table}) confirms this relation under isometric (constant-density) scaling. For non-isometric pairs, the relation requires generalization. The generalized form is the compactness ratio:
\[
k_d = \frac{I_1}{I_2} \cdot \left(\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)^3 = \frac{D_1}{D_2} = \frac{M_1 R_2}{M_2 R_1}
\]
equivalently expressible in $(I, \rho)$ coordinates as:
\[
\frac{\text{DTD}_1}{\text{DTD}_2} = \left(\frac{I_1}{I_2}\right)^{1/5} \cdot \left(\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right)^{3/10}
\]
(Equation~\ref{eq:ste_density}). These are two equivalent expressions of the generalized STE. The compactness-ratio form $k_d$ exposes the inertial-geometric structure of the relationship directly; the $(I, \rho)$ form makes explicit that compactness has two independent inputs --- inertial scale and density. The fifth-root inertia form is recovered as the constant-density limit of either expression. Given the masses and mean radii of any two systems, the full relative time dilation between them follows from $k_d$.

\subsection{Time Dilation Is Geometric, Not Gravitational}

Compactness $D = M/R$ is a geometric quantity: mass distributed over a length. The label ``gravitational time dilation'' attaches historically because the effect was first observed in gravitational contexts, but no field-theoretic gravity entered the derivations above. Gravity is the name given to the observable consequence of compactness; the underlying cause is the relative geometric-inertial structure of the systems being compared. Time dilation is a structural property of how matter is distributed, not a property mediated by a force.

\subsection{Time Dilation Is System-Relational, Not Personal}

No observer experiences their own time as anything but normal. Time dilation arises only when the temporal property of one inertial system is compared against another. It is a structural property of the relationship between systems, not of any single system in isolation. Nothing physically changes for any observer on their own terms; what is structured is how two systems compare. This is the framing that the STE makes natural and that the standard treatment, by privileging the observer's frame, often obscures.

\subsection{Special and General Relativity Are Projections of One Law}

The Schwarzschild time dilation formula is the STE evaluated under the constraint $M_1 = M_2$: same mass, different radii. The Lorentz time dilation formula is the STE evaluated under the constraint $R_1 = R_2$: same radius, different masses, with the velocity-based form connected to the radius-based form by the escape-velocity identity. Neither is a separate theory. Both are evaluations of the same compactness-based relation under different choices of which variable is held fixed. The general case --- both mass and radius varying, both densities varying --- requires the universal STE (Equation~\ref{eq:ste_density}). Special and General Relativity are not independent discoveries; they are two projections of a single law into the regimes where one or the other variable happens to be constrained.

\subsection{$G$ Cancels in Comparisons}

The gravitational constant $G$ appears identically in both halves of any time dilation ratio between two systems and therefore cancels structurally. The precision of comparative predictions in this framework is not bounded by the experimental uncertainty in $G$ ($\sim 6$ significant figures); it is bounded only by the precision of the mass and length inputs. Whatever the true value of $G$ --- including any joint scale-dependence with $c$ that preserves the combination $G/c^2$ --- comparative time dilation between two systems is exact at the precision of those inputs. The framework's comparative predictions are decoupled from the precision floor of $G$.

\subsection{DTD and GTD: Complementary Forms with Computational Advantages}

The gravitational time dilation factor $d\tau/dt = \sqrt{1 - D/D_{crit}}$ answers ``how much slower is this system's clock relative to flat spacetime?'' The DeGerlia time dilation $\text{DTD} = \sqrt{D/D_{crit}}$ answers ``how compact is this system relative to its collapse threshold?'' The two are algebraically related ($\text{GTD}^2 + \text{DTD}^2 = 1$, Equation~\ref{eq:pythagorean}) and describe the same physical time dilation from different angles.

DTD is the natural computational form. The standard $\sqrt{1 - r_s/R}$ buries the time dilation signal under unity, with the meaningful information confined to digits far from the leading $1$; DTD expresses the same physics directly, with the signal in the leading digits. DTD ratios also decompose cleanly:
\[
\frac{\text{DTD}_1}{\text{DTD}_2} = \sqrt{\frac{M_1}{M_2}} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{R_2}{R_1}}
\]
into independent mass and mean-radius contributions, a decomposition the $\sqrt{1-k}$ form does not admit. Calculations may be carried in DTD throughout and converted back to standard $d\tau/dt$ at the end via $\sqrt{1 - \text{DTD}^2} = d\tau/dt$ when needed. Having both representations available makes the framework more flexible than the standard treatment.

\subsection{The Schwarzschild Radius Is Not the Singularity}

Standard treatments often conflate the Schwarzschild radius with the singularity, treating both as locations where physics breaks down. This framework keeps them properly distinct.

The \textit{singularity} is the asymptote at $R \to 0$: the formal limit where compactness $D = M/R$ diverges to infinity. It is approached but never reached, and is the only point in the formulation where the framework itself is undefined.

The \textit{Schwarzschild radius} is the point where $D = D_{crit}$, equivalently where the escape velocity equals $c$. It is a meaningful threshold --- a milestone in the system's compactness --- but it is not a place where the framework fails. The standard $\sqrt{1 - r_s/R}$ form becomes imaginary at $R = r_s$ and forces a conflation between the threshold and the singularity. The DTD formulation remains real and well-defined throughout, including for $R < r_s$. The inertia--time dilation identities (Equations~\ref{eq:inertia_recovery}--\ref{eq:dtd_from_I}) continue to hold past the event horizon, depending only on ratios of real-valued quantities. The framework therefore gives a coherent description of the region between the Schwarzschild radius and $R \to 0$ --- the region that standard general relativity treats as undefined or ambiguous.

\subsection{Causality from Inertial Additivity}

Both rotational and linear inertia are strictly additive (Equations~\ref{eq:moi} and~\ref{eq:mass}). The temporal properties of any system are therefore wholly determined by its constituent matter, establishing that causality in this framework propagates exclusively from component to system. No top-down field assumption is required; the law operates at the level of mass distribution itself.

\subsection{Future Extensions}

The symmetric cases treated here are a subset of the framework's domain. The $(I, \rho)$ parameterization extends to arbitrary mass distributions, since moment of inertia is defined for any geometry. The $D_{crit}$ derivation and Schwarzschild correspondence assume spherical symmetry and would need to be redone for asymmetric systems. Rotating systems described by the Kerr metric \citep{kerr1963} and non-spherical mass distributions are natural extensions enabled by the framework but not derived in this paper. The framework's reach into weakly-gravitating and non-gravitational regimes follows from the same formulas returning vanishingly small but well-defined values for low compactness; the structural law does not break down. Cosmological implications follow if the framework extends consistently to light propagation, but those are the subject of separate work.

\noindent Under static radius, $\text{DTD}_1/\text{DTD}_2 = \sqrt{M_1/M_2}$: time dilation depends only on mass (Table~\ref{tab:pair_ratios}, column $R = R$).

\subsubsection*{Case 3: Constant Density}

When $M \propto R^3$ and $R_1 > R_2$:

\begin{align}
k &= \underbrace{\left(\frac{I_2}{I_1}\right)^{1/L}}_{L=5}
= \underbrace{\left(\frac{M_2 R_2^2}{M_1 R_1^2}\right)^{1/L}}_{L=5}
= \underbrace{\left(\frac{R_2^3 R_2^2}{R_1^3 R_1^2}\right)^{1/L}}_{L=5}
= \underbrace{\left(\frac{R_2^5}{R_1^5}\right)^{1/L}}_{L=5} \notag \\
k &= \left(\frac{R_2^5}{R_1^5}\right)^{1/5}
= \frac{R_2}{R_1}
\end{align}

\begin{mdframed}
\textbf{Example (Case 3):} $S_1$: $M_1 = 1 \times 10^{30}$\,kg, $R_1 = 1 \times 10^{8}$\,m. $S_2$: $M_2 = 1 \times 10^{27}$\,kg, $R_2 = 1 \times 10^{7}$\,m. Both at $\rho = 2.38732 \times 10^{5}$\,kg/m$^3$. Starting from the isometric equivalence ($M \propto R^3$, $L = 5$):
\begin{align}
k &= \frac{R_2}{R_1}
= \frac{1 \times 10^{7}}{1 \times 10^{8}}
= 1 \times 10^{-1}
= 1.00000 \times 10^{-1} \notag
\end{align}
Since $D = M/R \propto R^2$ at constant density, $k_2/k_1 = (R_2/R_1)^2 = k^2$ where $k_i = D_i/D_{crit}$:
\begin{align}
k_1 &= \frac{M_1/R_1}{D_{crit}}
= \frac{1 \times 10^{30}/1 \times 10^{8}}{6.73295 \times 10^{26}}
= 1.48523 \times 10^{-5} \notag \\
k_2 &= k_1 \cdot k^2
= 1.48523 \times 10^{-5} \times (1.00000 \times 10^{-1})^2 \notag \\
&= 1.48523 \times 10^{-5} \times 1.00000 \times 10^{-2}
= 1.48523 \times 10^{-7} \notag \\
\frac{d\tau}{dt} &= \frac{\sqrt{1 - k_1}}{\sqrt{1 - k_2}}
= \frac{\sqrt{1 - 1.48523 \times 10^{-5}}}{\sqrt{1 - 1.48523 \times 10^{-7}}}
= 9.9999264807 \times 10^{-1} \notag
\end{align}
\textbf{Confirmation:} Schwarzschild gravitational time dilation:
\begin{align}
\frac{d\tau}{dt} &= \frac{\sqrt{1 - \frac{2GM_1}{R_1 c^2}}}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{2GM_2}{R_2 c^2}}} \notag \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{1 - \frac{2(6.67430 \times 10^{-11})(1 \times 10^{30})}{(1 \times 10^{8})(8.98755 \times 10^{16})}}}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{2(6.67430 \times 10^{-11})(1 \times 10^{27})}{(1 \times 10^{7})(8.98755 \times 10^{16})}}} \notag \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{1 - 1.48523 \times 10^{-5}}}{\sqrt{1 - 1.48523 \times 10^{-7}}}
= 9.9999264807 \times 10^{-1} \notag
\end{align}
\end{mdframed}

\noindent This is the only constraint under which $(I_1/I_2)^{1/5} = \text{DTD}_1/\text{DTD}_2$; the density correction $(\rho_1/\rho_2)^{3/10}$ in Equation~\ref{eq:ste_density} is unity (Table~\ref{tab:pair_ratios}, column $\rho = \rho$).

\subsubsection*{Case 4: Constant Inertia}

When $M_1 R_1^2 = M_2 R_2^2$:

\begin{align}
k &= \underbrace{\left(\frac{I_2}{I_1}\right)^{1/L}}_{L=5} = \underbrace{\left(\frac{M_2 R_2^2}{M_1 R_1^2}\right)^{1/L}}_{L=5} \notag \\
k &= \left(\frac{M_2 R_2^2}{M_1 R_1^2}\right)^{1/5} = (1)^{1/5} = 1
\end{align}

\begin{mdframed}
\textbf{Example (Case 4):} $S_1$: $M_1 = 1 \times 10^{30}$\,kg, $R_1 = 1 \times 10^{8}$\,m. $S_2$: $M_2 = 1 \times 10^{32}$\,kg, $R_2 = 1 \times 10^{7}$\,m. Both have $I = MR^2 = 1 \times 10^{46}$\,kg$\cdot$m$^2$. Starting from the isometric equivalence:
\begin{align}
I_1 &= M_1 R_1^2 = (1 \times 10^{30})(1 \times 10^{8})^2 = 1 \times 10^{46} \notag \\
I_2 &= M_2 R_2^2 = (1 \times 10^{32})(1 \times 10^{7})^2 = 1 \times 10^{46} \notag \\
k &= \left(\frac{M_2 R_2^2}{M_1 R_1^2}\right)^{1/5}
= \left(\frac{1 \times 10^{46}}{1 \times 10^{46}}\right)^{1/5}
= 1 \notag
\end{align}
\textbf{Confirmation:} Computing each system's $D_{norm}$ independently:
\begin{align}
k_1 &= \frac{D_1}{D_{crit}} = \frac{M_1/R_1}{D_{crit}}
= \frac{1 \times 10^{30}/1 \times 10^{8}}{6.73295 \times 10^{26}}
= 1.48523 \times 10^{-5} \notag \\
k_2 &= \frac{D_2}{D_{crit}} = \frac{M_2/R_2}{D_{crit}}
= \frac{1 \times 10^{32}/1 \times 10^{7}}{6.73295 \times 10^{26}}
= 1.48523 \times 10^{-2} \notag \\
\text{DTD}_1 &= \sqrt{k_1} = 3.85387 \times 10^{-3} \notag \\
\text{DTD}_2 &= \sqrt{k_2} = 1.21870 \times 10^{-1} \notag \\
\frac{\text{DTD}_2}{\text{DTD}_1} &= 3.16228 \times 10^{1} \notag \\
\left(\frac{\text{DTD}_2}{\text{DTD}_1}\right)^2 \cdot \left(\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)^3
&= (3.16228 \times 10^{1})^2 \cdot \left(\frac{1 \times 10^{7}}{1 \times 10^{8}}\right)^3
= 1.00000 \times 10^{3} \times 1.00000 \times 10^{-3}
= 1.00000 \times 10^{0}
= \frac{I_2}{I_1} \notag
\end{align}
\end{mdframed}

\noindent Equal inertia does not imply equal compactness: here $(I_2/I_1)^{1/5} = 1$ while the DTD ratio is $31.62$, confirming Equation~\ref{eq:inertia_recovery} (Table~\ref{tab:pair_ratios}, column $I = I$).

\subsubsection*{General Case}

When $M_1 \neq M_2$, $R_1 \neq R_2$, $M_1 \neq R_1^3$, and $M_2 \neq R_2^3$:

\begin{align}
k &= \underbrace{\left(\frac{I_2}{I_1}\right)^{1/L}}_{L=5} = \underbrace{\left(\frac{M_2 R_2^2}{M_1 R_1^2}\right)^{1/L}}_{L=5} = \underbrace{\left(\frac{M_2}{M_1}\right)^{1/L}}_{L=5} \cdot \underbrace{\left(\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)^{2/L}}_{L=5} \notag \\
k &= \left(\frac{M_2 R_2^2}{M_1 R_1^2}\right)^{1/5} = \left(\frac{M_2}{M_1}\right)^{1/5} \cdot \left(\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)^{2/5}
\end{align}

\begin{mdframed}
\textbf{Example (General Case):} $S_1$: $M_1 = 1 \times 10^{30}$\,kg, $R_1 = 1 \times 10^{7}$\,m. $S_2$: $M_2 = 1 \times 10^{20}$\,kg, $R_2 = 2 \times 10^{-7}$\,m. Starting from the isometric equivalence:
\begin{align}
k &= \left(\frac{M_2 R_2^2}{M_1 R_1^2}\right)^{1/5}
= \left(\frac{(1 \times 10^{20}) \cdot (2 \times 10^{-7})^2}{(1 \times 10^{30}) \cdot (1 \times 10^{7})^2}\right)^{1/5} \notag \\
&= \left(\frac{4.00000 \times 10^{6}}{1 \times 10^{44}}\right)^{1/5}
= (4.00000 \times 10^{-38})^{1/5} \notag \\
&= 3.31445 \times 10^{-8} \notag
\end{align}
Since $k_i = D_i/D_{crit}$ and $D = M/R$:
\begin{align}
k_1 &= \frac{M_1/R_1}{D_{crit}}
= \frac{1 \times 10^{30}/1 \times 10^{7}}{6.73295 \times 10^{26}}
= 1.48523 \times 10^{-4} \notag \\
k_2 &= \frac{M_2/R_2}{D_{crit}}
= \frac{1 \times 10^{20}/2 \times 10^{-7}}{6.73295 \times 10^{26}}
= 7.42617 \times 10^{-1} \notag
\end{align}
\begin{align}
\frac{d\tau}{dt} &= \frac{\sqrt{1 - k_2}}{\sqrt{1 - k_1}}
= \frac{\sqrt{1 - 7.42617 \times 10^{-1}}}{\sqrt{1 - 1.48523 \times 10^{-4}}} \notag \\
&= 5.07368 \times 10^{-1} \notag
\end{align}
\textbf{Confirmation:} Schwarzschild gravitational time dilation:
\begin{align}
\frac{d\tau}{dt} &= \frac{\sqrt{1 - \frac{2GM_2}{R_2 c^2}}}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{2GM_1}{R_1 c^2}}} \notag \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{1 - \frac{2(6.67430 \times 10^{-11})(1 \times 10^{20})}{(2 \times 10^{-7})(8.98755 \times 10^{16})}}}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{2(6.67430 \times 10^{-11})(1 \times 10^{30})}{(1 \times 10^{7})(8.98755 \times 10^{16})}}} \notag \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{1 - 7.42617 \times 10^{-1}}}{\sqrt{1 - 1.48523 \times 10^{-4}}} \notag \\
&= 5.07368 \times 10^{-1} \notag
\end{align}
\end{mdframed}

\noindent All identities from Section~5.5 hold without constraint; numerical verification appears in Table~\ref{tab:pair_ratios}, column General.

\begin{mdframed}
\textbf{Example (General Case --- Comparison of Relative Time Paces):} $S_1$: $M_1 = 1 \times 10^{30}$\,kg, $R_1 = 1 \times 10^{7}$\,m. $S_2$: $M_2 = 1 \times 10^{20}$\,kg, $R_2 = 2 \times 10^{-7}$\,m.

Linear scale factor (Equation~\ref{eq:kd}):
\begin{align}
k &= \frac{M_1 R_2}{M_2 R_1}
= \frac{(1 \times 10^{30})(2 \times 10^{-7})}{(1 \times 10^{20})(1 \times 10^{7})}
= 2.00000 \times 10^{-4} \notag \\
D_{norm} &= \frac{M_2/R_2}{D_{crit}}
= \frac{1 \times 10^{20}/2 \times 10^{-7}}{6.73295 \times 10^{26}}
= 7.42617 \times 10^{-1} \notag \\
\frac{d\tau}{dt} &= \sqrt{1 - D_{norm}}
= \sqrt{1 - 7.42617 \times 10^{-1}}
= 5.07330 \times 10^{-1} \notag
\end{align}
\textbf{Confirmation:} Schwarzschild gravitational time dilation for each system:

$S_1$:
\begin{align}
\frac{d\tau}{dt} &= \sqrt{1 - \frac{2(6.67430 \times 10^{-11})(1 \times 10^{30})}{(1 \times 10^{7})(8.98755 \times 10^{16})}}
= \sqrt{1 - 1.48523 \times 10^{-4}}
= 9.99926 \times 10^{-1} \notag
\end{align}
$S_2$:
\begin{align}
\frac{d\tau}{dt} &= \sqrt{1 - \frac{2(6.67430 \times 10^{-11})(1 \times 10^{20})}{(2 \times 10^{-7})(8.98755 \times 10^{16})}}
= \sqrt{1 - 7.42617 \times 10^{-1}}
= 5.07330 \times 10^{-1} \notag
\end{align}
Relative pace of time:
\begin{align}
\frac{\sqrt{1 - k_2}}{\sqrt{1 - k_1}} &= \frac{5.07330 \times 10^{-1}}{9.99926 \times 10^{-1}}
= 5.07368 \times 10^{-1} \notag
\end{align}
\end{mdframed}
